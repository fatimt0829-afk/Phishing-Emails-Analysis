# Project Reflection

Before this project, I understood that phishing involved fake emails, but I wanted to understand why people still fall for them. I learned that the message itself is only one part of the attack. The other part is social engineering: using fear, urgency, authority, curiosity, or rewards to influence someone's decision.

The most useful habit I learned was to pause before taking action. A phishing email often tries to make the reader feel like there is no time to check. Looking at the full sender address, thinking about whether the request makes sense, and contacting the organization another way can reveal that something is wrong.

I also learned that phishing emails do not always contain bad spelling or look obviously fake. Some can look clean and professional, so it is better to judge the sender and the request instead of trusting the design.

If I received a suspicious email, I would avoid clicking anything, verify the request through an official source, report the email, and follow my school or workplace's security process.

This project helped me practice security awareness and risk recognition. In a future project, I would like to learn more about how organizations train employees and build procedures for reporting suspicious messages.
