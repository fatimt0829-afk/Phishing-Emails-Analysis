# Phishing and Social Engineering Guide

## What is phishing?

Phishing is when an attacker sends a fake message that looks like it came from a trusted source. The goal is usually to convince someone to click a link, open a file, send money, or reveal information such as a password.

Phishing is commonly delivered through email, but similar scams can also appear in text messages, social media messages, and phone calls.

## How does phishing connect to social engineering?

Social engineering means manipulating a person into doing something that helps an attacker. Instead of breaking through a technical security control, the attacker tries to influence the person's decision.

Phishing is one form of social engineering. The message is the method, while the pressure or deception inside the message is what influences the target.

Common tactics include:

| Tactic | What it tries to make the reader feel |
| --- | --- |
| Urgency | “I need to act before I have time to think.” |
| Fear | “Something bad will happen if I ignore this.” |
| Authority | “This came from a boss, teacher, bank, or IT department, so I should obey.” |
| Curiosity | “I want to know what is inside the link or attachment.” |
| Reward | “I might win money, a gift, or another benefit.” |

## Common phishing indicators

### 1. A strange sender address

The display name may look familiar while the actual email address is unrelated, misspelled, or from an unusual domain.

### 2. Urgent or threatening language

The message may claim an account will be closed, a payment is overdue, or immediate action is required. This pressure is meant to stop the reader from checking first.

### 3. Requests for sensitive information

Legitimate organizations normally should not ask for a password, verification code, banking information, or other sensitive information through an unexpected email.

### 4. Unexpected links or attachments

A message may ask the reader to sign in, review a document, track a package, or open an invoice. If the message was not expected, the link or file should not be opened.

### 5. A link that does not match the organization

The words shown in a link may look official, but the actual destination can be different. On a computer, hovering over a link can show its destination without opening it.

### 6. An unusual request from someone familiar

A message can pretend to be a teacher, manager, friend, or family member. A sudden request for money, gift cards, login information, or secrecy should be confirmed another way.

### 7. Offers that seem too good to be true

Unexpected prizes, refunds, jobs, or giveaways can be used to make someone act before thinking.

### 8. Spelling or formatting problems

Some phishing emails contain obvious mistakes, but others look polished. Good grammar does not automatically make an email safe.

## What to do with a suspicious email

1. Do not click the link, open the attachment, or reply.
2. Check the full sender address.
3. Visit the organization's official website by typing the address yourself or using a trusted bookmark.
4. Contact the person or organization through a phone number, website, or account you already know.
5. Report the message using the email provider's phishing option or the organization's reporting process.
6. Delete the message after it has been reported, if instructed.

If a link was already clicked or information was entered, the user should report it quickly, change the affected password from the real website, and follow the organization's security instructions.

## Important reminder

No single warning sign proves that a message is phishing. The safest decision comes from looking at the full situation: the sender, the message, the request, and whether it makes sense.
