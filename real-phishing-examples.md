# Real Phishing Email Examples

These are two suspicious emails that were actually sent to my inbox. I did not click the links or interact with the senders. I analyzed only what was visible in the messages.

## Example 1: Fake cloud-storage warning

![Screenshot of a suspicious cloud-storage warning](images/01-storage-alert-phishing-email.png)

### What the email was trying to do

The message claimed that my account had been blocked and that my photos and videos would be deleted that night. It also told me to renew a subscription. The goal was to make the situation feel urgent enough that I would act before checking whether the warning was real.

### Warning signs I noticed

1. **Random sender address:** The sender address is a long, unusual string that does not match a recognizable cloud-storage company.
2. **Unrelated sending domain:** Gmail shows that the message was sent through another unrelated domain.
3. **Gmail placed it in spam:** Gmail says the message is similar to messages previously identified as spam. This supports the other warning signs, although a spam label by itself is not final proof.
4. **Fear and urgency:** Phrases such as “blocked,” “deleted tonight,” and the exact deletion deadline are meant to cause panic.
5. **No real company name:** The email uses the general phrase “Cloud Storage” instead of clearly identifying a service I use.
6. **Confusing offer:** The subject says to renew a subscription “for free,” which does not make much sense.
7. **Pressure to take action:** The message focuses on a supposed immediate loss instead of giving a normal way to confirm the account status.

### Social engineering tactic

**Fear and urgency.** The sender wants the reader to imagine losing important photos and act quickly.

### My decision

This email has strong phishing and scam indicators. I would not use anything inside the message. I would open the real storage app or official website separately and check the account there. I would then report the email as phishing and delete it.

## Example 2: Fake Venmo offer

![Screenshot of a suspicious Venmo offer](images/02-venmo-offer-phishing-email.png)

### What the email was trying to do

The message claimed that I had received a Venmo offer from a contact. Instead of showing a clear payment, it used buttons such as “View cash value” and “Accept Venmo” to move me to another page.

### Warning signs I noticed

1. **Blank greeting:** The message begins with “Dear,” but does not include my name.
2. **Unexpected money:** I was not expecting a Venmo offer from anyone.
3. **Missing amount:** The email does not show how much money was supposedly sent.
4. **Unclear sender:** The “From” line only says “-support,” which does not identify a real person.
5. **Vague wording:** “Venmo offer” is unclear compared with a normal payment notification.
6. **Request to continue elsewhere:** It says I need to confirm details on the next page and answer questions.
7. **Curiosity-based buttons:** “View cash value” hides the supposed amount until the reader clicks.
8. **Strange confirmation number:** The large button includes a random-looking code, which may be there to make the offer look official.

### Social engineering tactic

**Reward and curiosity.** The sender wants the reader to focus on the possibility of receiving money instead of questioning the message.

### My decision

This email also has strong phishing and scam indicators. I would not press either button. I would open the Venmo app directly and check whether a real payment appeared in my account. If nothing appeared, I would report and delete the message.

## Comparing the two emails

| Question | Cloud-storage email | Venmo email |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Main emotion used | Fear | Excitement and curiosity |
| What it promises or threatens | Loss of photos and account access | Unexpected money |
| Personal greeting | No | No |
| Clear, trustworthy sender | No | No |
| Safest verification method | Open the real storage account separately | Open the Venmo app directly |

## Conclusion

These examples showed me that phishing can use opposite emotions. The first email threatens a loss, while the second promises a reward. Both try to make the reader click before verifying the situation.
